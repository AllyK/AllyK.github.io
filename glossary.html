Abhyanga

abhyaṅga

Self-massage with warm oil, one of the durable pieces of the daily routine. Whatever else it is, it is touch and unhurried time.

Agni

agni

The digestive fire, and the hinge of the whole Ayurvedic system. Strong agni turns ordinary food into ojas; weak agni leaves ama behind. Ayurveda asks what a person can digest before it asks what they eat.

Wisdomlib ↗Ayurveda

Ahara

Food, and the system’s primary medicine. The classical logic is like increases like, and opposites balance, applied through the qualities of what is eaten.

Wisdomlib ↗Ayurveda

Ahimsa

ahimsa

Non-harming: the first yama in Patanjali's Yoga Sutras. Arjuna's refusal to fight is framed initially as ahimsa. The Bhagavad Gita reframes it: the deepest harm can be the refusal of one's own dharma. The soul cannot be killed; grief at action is not the same as grief at harm.

Wisdomlib ↗Yama

Akasha

akasha

Space or ether: the subtlest of the five great elements and the medium of sound. In later esoteric traditions, the "akashic" field of subtle impression. The element through which the metaphysical reaches open.

Wisdomlib ↗Meditation

Ama

āma

The undigested residue left when digestion is weak. The word means raw or uncooked, and the classical texts blame ama for a long list of illnesses.

Wisdomlib ↗Ayurveda

Anatta

anatta / anatman

In Buddhism, not-self: the teaching that no permanent, unchanging Self can be found. Where the Vedic tradition finds atman, the Buddha found a flowing process with no fixed owner. The great divergence between the two streams.

Anga bhanga sadhana

aṅga bhaṅga sādhanā

A “limb-harming practice”: Krishnamacharya’s phrase, as Gary Kraftsow reports it, for any practice that helps one part of us while harming another. His example was running, good for the heart and lungs and hard on many knees and hips. Any practice, yoga included, can become one when it is wrongly applied.

Ayurveda

ayus + veda

The knowledge of life: a complete medical system with its own canon, institutions, and history, older as a written tradition than Patanjali’s eight limbs. It is often called yoga’s sister science, which holds as long as sister means a separate person.

Bandha

bandha

A “lock,” a muscular contraction held to contain and redirect the breath and its energy. The Bihar School glosses the word as to “hold,” “tighten” or “lock,” and explains that “the bandhas aim to lock the pranas in particular areas and redirect their flow into sushumna nadi for the purpose of spiritual awakening.” The three classic locks are mula bandha at the root, jalandhara bandha at the throat, and uddiyana bandha at the abdomen.

Bhagavad Gita

bhagavad gita

The divine song: 700 verses spoken by Krishna to Arjuna on the battlefield of Kurukshetra, embedded within the Mahabharata. The central teaching is nishkama karma, act fully, without attachment to outcomes. It is the most-cited scripture in the yoga tradition.

Wisdomlib ↗Under the Banyan Tree

Bhakti

bhakti

Devotional service: the yoga of love. Understood in the Bhagavatam as the highest form of practice because it operates through the heart. Prahlada's story is its primary teaching text. Alongside the familiar sense of loving devotion, the word carries an older, more concrete range inherited from its Sanskrit verbal root bhaj-, which the lexicons gloss as "distribution, partition, separation" as well as attachment, so that a share, a portion, and a belonging are all folded into the idea of devotion.

Bhuta-shuddhi

bhuta-śuddhi

The “purification of the elements,” a preparatory practice in the tantric traditions. The scholar Georg Feuerstein describes it as “an important Tantric practice and a precondition for the safe and complete arousal of the serpent power (kundalini-shakti).”

The awakened heart-mind of Mahayana Buddhism: the aspiration to awaken for the sake of all beings, and the felt kinship beneath the separate self. As Jack Kornfield glosses it, “This sense of deep connection, of belonging to the same family, is bodhichitta.”

Brahmana

A practice that nourishes and energizes: it favors the inhale and the pause after it, backbends and lunges, and a quicker pace. The yoga tradition pairs it with langhana, its calming counterpart. Gheranda Samhita

gheranda-samhita

A classical Sanskrit manual of Hatha Yoga, framed as a dialogue in which the sage Gheranda answers the pupil Chanda Kapali. It organizes practice into seven exercises rather than Patanjali’s eight limbs: the “purificatory, strengthening, steadying, calming, and those leading to lightness, perception, and isolation” (I.2), and it teaches that of the many asanas “thirty-two have been found useful for mankind in this world” (II). Scholars count it, with the Hatha Yoga Pradipika and the Shiva Samhita, among the three seminal texts of hatha yoga.

Jnana Yoga

jñāna · knowledge

The path of self-knowledge, one of the four classical yogas alongside bhakti, karma, and raja. It works by viveka (discrimination) and inquiry, the steady negation neti, neti (not this, not this), until every false identification falls away and only the witnessing Self remains. Its classic texts are the Upanishads and the Yoga Vasistha. The eight limbs are raja yoga’s method; the four paths are interwoven and may be practiced together or apart.

Klesha

The five afflictions that keep the mind bound and drive suffering (Yoga Sutra 2.3–9). The root of them is avidya, spiritual ignorance; as Georg Feuerstein sums up Patanjali, “This is the primary klesha. From it spring the I-sense (asmita), attachment (raga), aversion (dvesha), and the will to live (abhinivesha).” Kriya-yoga aims at their gradual thinning.

Kumbhaka

kumbhaka

Breath retention, the held pause of pranayama. In the words of the Hatha Yoga Pradipika, “Kumbhaka is the keeping the air confined inside” (HYP 2.45, trans. Pancham Sinh). It is the phase in which the bandhas (locks) are engaged, and the classical texts distinguish retention after the in-breath from retention after the out-breath.

Kundalini

In tantric and hatha yoga, a dormant spiritual energy pictured as coiled at the base of the spine, at muladhara, and awakened to rise through the central channel. The common derivation, as Swami Satyananda notes, is from kundal, a coil, so kundalini is “that which is coiled.” This is a subtle-body concept of yogic experience, not a claim about physical anatomy.

Langhana

A practice that calms and reduces: it lengthens the exhale and favors forward folds, twists, and a slower pace, suited to someone carrying stress or agitation. Nadi

nāḍī

A channel of the subtle body through which prana, the life-force, flows. Georg Feuerstein locates this “network of channels along which flows the life force” in the body’s subtle counterpart rather than the physical body, traditionally numbered at 72,000, of which three are chief: ida, pingala, and sushumna. The tantric writer Shyam Sundar Goswami stresses that the nadis “do not exist as tubules or wires, but are subtle lines of direction” along which the vital airs move, so they are energy pathways rather than nerves or vessels. Ida is read as the lunar channel on the left of the spine and pingala as the solar channel on the right; the Shiva Samhita, in Goswami’s rendering, names fourteen chief nadis in all.

Nirvana

In Buddhism, the “blowing out” of the fires of craving, aversion, and delusion, and the liberation and peace that follow. Pancha Kosha

pancha kosha

The five sheaths that cover the Self, set out in the Taittiriya Upanishad (Brahmananda Valli): the physical (annamaya), the energetic or breath (pranamaya), the mental (manomaya), the wisdom (vijnanamaya), and the bliss (anandamaya) bodies. Meditation peels them back, layer by layer, toward the center. The Upanishad pictures even that innermost bliss-body in the shape of a person: “joy is the right wing, delight is the left wing, Bliss is the self, Brahman is the tail, the support.” The peeling does not end at bliss; beneath it lies Brahman, the ground on which the sheaths rest. In yoga therapy, Gary Kraftsow uses the same five dimensions as a map of the whole person, since a condition in one layer reaches all the others.

Samatva

Evenness of mind; equanimity. The Gita's working definition of yoga: samatvam yoga uchyate, evenness is yoga (2.48). The same steadiness in success and failure, gain and loss.

Samsara

samsara

The round of birth, death, and rebirth, turned by karma and the mind's clinging. It is the condition that moksha releases, and most of the older texts assume it as the backdrop against which liberation makes sense. The contemporary Zen teacher Thich Nhat Hanh reads it moment to moment rather than lifetime to lifetime: "Karma is our continuation, and continuation is rebirth. Every moment is a moment of rebirth."

One's own specific path of duty, distinct from dharma in general. The Bhagavad Gita holds that it is better to fulfill one's own swadharma imperfectly than another's perfectly. A deep-seated behavioral trait, the accumulated residue of past impressions. Where a single samskara is one subconscious imprint left by an act of will, a vasana is the whole chain of them. Vata

Wind or current. The collective name is pancha prana.
