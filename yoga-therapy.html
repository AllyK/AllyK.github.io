Practice Shaped to the Person

A first practice: spine and breath

Kraftsow’s general rule is to begin every therapeutic practice with the spine and the breath, because linking breath to movement links the mind to the body and makes the movement conscious. This short sequence follows that rule and suits almost any starting point. We move in and out of each shape with the breath several times before staying.

  1. Arrive lying on the back, knees bent, feet on the floor. For six breaths we simply notice where each inhale begins and where each exhale ends.
  2. Arms overhead on the inhale, back down on the exhale, six times. The inhale widens the chest from the top down; the arms follow the breath.
  3. Apanasana. Hands on the knees, we draw the knees toward the chest on the exhale, gently firming the belly from the pubic bone up toward the navel, and let them move away on the inhale. Six times.
  4. Cakravakasana, on hands and knees. On the exhale we fold back toward the heels, rounding the back; on the inhale we come forward and lift the chest. Six times, slowly, with the breath leading.
  5. Rest on the back for several breaths, letting the exhale grow a little longer than the inhale.
The book

Function over form

Gary Kraftsow began studying with T.K.V. Desikachar in India in 1974 and founded the American Viniyoga Institute in 1999, the year Penguin published Yoga for Wellness. The book sets down the therapeutic side of the Viniyoga tradition that Desikachar carried from his father, Tirumalai Krishnamacharya, the same teacher at the root of the lineage tree.

The book rests on one idea. In Kraftsow’s reading of the tradition, the way each person did a posture was worked out between teacher and student, and

“the transformational value of a posture was always seen in relation to its function, not to its form.”Gary Kraftsow, Yoga for Wellness (1999), Introduction

A posture has value for what it does in a particular body, and so the form is free to change. That idea turns a set of fixed shapes into a set of tools, and it is the ground of everything below. Kraftsow ties it to samskara, the conditioning that repetition lays into how we move and react. Habit keeps us functioning and also limits us. Turning attention inward, and moving with awareness led by the breath, is how new patterns begin to replace old ones.

The same idea runs through the adaptation principles I learned in my September 2026 training, set out in Adapting the pose on the sequencing page. Reading Kraftsow showed me the lineage those principles come from.

How to do it

Six steps of a therapeutic practice

Kraftsow presents therapy as a process between two people more than a menu of poses for conditions. I read his method as six steps, each drawn from his own chapters. The sequences in his book are, in his words, “descriptive rather than prescriptive”: models of an approach, meant to be adapted again for each person.

1Meet the whole person

Kraftsow maps a person in five dimensions, the pancamaya, or five sheaths, of the Taittiriya Upanishad: the physical body, the breath and vital energy, the mind, the deeper layer of understanding, values, and past patterns (vijnana maya), and the heart, our sense of joy and what we cherish (ananda maya). A condition in one dimension reaches all the others, so long-term back pain can bring fatigue and low mood, and low self-worth can end in joint pain. The first work is listening across all five.

He places the relationship ahead of the technique: “the technology of practice is secondary to, and emerges out of, the relationship that develops in the environment of the session between the teacher and the student.” The tradition’s word for right association is sat sangha.

2Screen before practice

For the neck, shoulders, and back, Kraftsow names the signs that call for a professional diagnosis before any yoga: numbness or tingling in the arms, hands, legs, or feet; sharp, electric, or immobilizing pain; sharp pain in the shoulder. In those conditions, he writes, yoga may assist healing, and the wrong practice may make matters worse. For persistent anxiety, panic, obsessive-compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress, or deep depression, he recommends professional psychiatric care, with yoga as a companion to it.

3Choose a direction

Every therapeutic practice leans one of two ways, described in the pair of cards below. Kraftsow adds a third lens from the three gunas: agitation (rajas), as in anger and anxiety, calls for calming; dullness (tamas), as in depression, calls for awakening interest and energy; and the aim of both is clarity and balance (sattva). The two directions often mix. For a person with chronic fatigue, his sequence begins gently building to raise energy and ends gently calming, because too much heat would leave her exhausted. For anxiety he meets the person where the mind is: first movement strong enough to match the agitation, then a gradual slowing, deep slow breathing, sound, and finally meditation or prayer.

4Start where the person is

Kraftsow pictures health on a scale from minus one, a very serious condition, to ten, optimal health, and starts each person at a different point on it. For serious illness the practice begins lying on a bed or seated in a chair, with awareness of the breath. From there, the practice starts with simple movements of the spine linked to breath, and adds techniques as the person is ready.

5Adapt and sequence

This is the craft at the center of the book: changing posture, breath, sound, and props to fit the person, then ordering the practice so that each part prepares for the next and the body returns to neutral at the end. The toolkit and the sequencing principles have their own sections below.

6Practice, watch, and revise

“Without practice, there will be no results,” Kraftsow writes, and the practice has to change as the person changes. His teacher Krishnamacharya had a name for any practice that helps one part of us while harming another: anga bhanga sadhana, literally a limb-harming practice. His example was running (“Running is for horses!”), good for the heart and lungs and hard on many ankles, knees, and hips. Any practice, a yoga practice included, can become one when it is wrongly applied, so part of the ongoing work is watching whether a practice still serves the person doing it.

Building

brahmana (bṛṃhaṇa), tonification

For weakness, low energy, specific debilities, lack of confidence, and recovery after illness. The techniques are the inhale, holding after the inhale, backbends, and shoulderstand, and chanting, visualization, and meditation can be shaped the same way. Building practice generally raises heat and energy.

Reducing

langhana, reduction and purification

For conditions of excess such as hyperactivity or anxiety. The techniques are the exhale, a short hold after the exhale, forward bends, some twists, and headstand. Reducing practice is generally cooling and conserves energy, and Kraftsow notes it can also serve as prevention.

The toolkit

Ways to adapt a practice

Kraftsow gives three reasons to adapt: to keep practice from becoming mechanical, to meet conditions that change from day to day, and to work with long-term problems. Adaptation is also how we emphasize different effects of the same pose. His tools fall into four groups.

The form of the posture

Arms

One arm instead of two lightens the load, eases the neck and shoulders, and works each side on its own. Sweeping arms do the same, and paired with head movements they stretch and strengthen the neck. Bending and straightening the arms brings awareness to the upper back and helps it flatten. Using the arms as levers takes us deeper, with more risk to the joints.

Legs

Bent knees in standing poses lower the center of gravity and let the low back stretch; in seated poses they can help or limit the pelvis tipping forward. Wider feet increase the stretch in standing and seated poses, and ask more of the hip muscles on the back and belly. Shifting one knee back deepens the stretch on that side of the back.

Props

A wall, table, chair, blocks, cushions, straps, and light weights, used when movement is restricted or to intensify work in one area. Kraftsow shows a twist on a chair, a kneeling pose against a wall, and a book under the hips in a forward bend.

The movement into and out of it

Origin

Where a movement begins shapes its effect. Beginning a standing forward bend from the belly limits the forward tip of the pelvis; beginning it from the hips invites it.

Direction

Coming up into Ardha Uttanasana helps flatten the upper back, which suits a normal to rounded upper back. Moving down into it rounds the upper back, which suits a flatter one. In Bhujangasana, pushing down through the hands deepens the arch in the low back, while pulling back on the hands and bringing the chest forward deepens it in the upper back.

Range

Going as far as possible is often the wrong aim. A person who drops easily into Upavistha Konasana stretches the low back far more by bending the knees and keeping the sit bones down.

Stages, speed, and staying

Breaking a movement into stages (krama) deepens it. Faster movement energizes and slower movement calms, always paced to a full breath. Moving in and out of a pose several times usually prepares us to stay in it.

Attention

Where we place attention changes the pose. Kraftsow suggests moving into Parsvottanasana four times, attending first to the back heel, then the belly, then the hands, then all three. Holding a quality such as kindness in mind can soften the whole practice.

The breath

In this tradition the breath always begins before the movement and leads it: “first feel the current, and then swim with it.” On the inhale the chest expands from the top down, and on the exhale the belly firms from the pubic bone up toward the navel, which steadies the pelvis and low back. The breath itself can then be adapted, and the breath page holds the fuller practices.

Length and ratio

Fix the length of the inhale or exhale, set a ratio between them, or lengthen both while keeping the ratio. Kraftsow notes that the exhale is commonly longer than the inhale, and only rarely the reverse.

Holds

Holding after the inhale extends the inhale’s effect and suits poses that emphasize the inhale. Holding after the exhale extends the exhale’s effect. Both intensify a posture.

Technique and pattern

If the upper back and neck are stiff, focusing the inhale at the solar plexus can ease tension. A movement usually done on the inhale can be done on the exhale, and the breath can be taken in segments (krama breathing), pausing between each third.

Sound

Humming, chanting single syllables, phrases with meaning, or whole songs and prayers focus attention and lengthen the exhale. Kraftsow reports broad agreement that higher-pitched sounds resonate higher in the body and energize, lower sounds resonate lower and calm, louder sounds turn attention outward, and softer sounds turn it inward. He also invites us to test this in our own bodies.

Putting it in order

Preparing and counteracting

Two principles order every part of a Viniyoga practice. Vinyasa, “arranging” or “placing,” moves from the gross to the subtle, the external to the internal, the simple to the complex, the easy to the difficult: asana prepares the breath for pranayama, and pranayama prepares the mind for meditation. Pratikriya, “counteracting,” returns the body, breath, and mind to neutral by moving the other way.

The counterpose depends on the person and the day. After a deep backbend one person needs the low back and hips settled first, another the upper back, neck, and shoulders. The same posture, placed after different preparations, also does different work: Kraftsow prepares for Pascimatanasana with forward bends for someone with tight hips, and with backbends for someone whose hips and low back are loose.

Seven principles for designing a sequence

Intention: set a goal for the practice. Efficiency: keep the number of poses and adaptations small and purposeful; too many dilute the effect. Breath: let the breath link attention to the spine, and use holds on purpose. Transition: take time to arrive, and use kneeling poses to move between standing and lying down. Cumulative stress: compensate for strain gathering in the neck, shoulders, hips, and knees. Risk: place riskier postures later, after preparation, followed by simple counterposes. Rest: rest when the breath grows unsteady, after hard poses, when changing direction, and at length at the end.

Forward bends as the hub

In Kraftsow’s account, forward bending is the hub of the wheel and backbends, twists, and side bends are its spokes, so a forward bend carries the practice from one direction to the next. Standing and kneeling poses warm the body, poses on the back relax and prepare for inversions, headstand and shoulderstand sit between half and two thirds of the way through, poses on the stomach strengthen the back, and seated poses come near the end, preparing for pranayama and meditation.

Shaped to the day

A practice should fit the day around it. Someone tired in the morning might begin lying down, rise through the knees to standing, and end resting on a chair before work. Someone wound up in the evening might begin standing and travel down through the knees to lying on the back before sleep. Physical work calls for a practice that restores balance; desk work calls for one that moves the body and clears the mind.

What the book covers

Three kinds of condition

The therapeutic half of the book applies this method in three chapters, each with case studies from Kraftsow’s own students and the practices he built for them.

Common aches and pains

The skeletal and muscular systems, then the neck and shoulders; the upper and lower back; and the sacrum, hips, and knees.

Chronic disease

Digestion, the respiratory, cardiovascular, lymphatic and immune, endocrine, and nervous systems, and the urinary and reproductive systems, each read as conditions of excess or deficiency.

Emotional health

Anger and anxiety as states of overarousal, depression as a state of underarousal, and the practices of breath, sound, reframing (pratipaksha bhavana), and meditation that meet each.

An example: the neck and shoulders

Kraftsow reads most neck and shoulder problems as a poor working relationship among the head, neck, shoulders, arms, rib cage, and upper back, and he works the neck by changing that relationship. Our habit is for the head and shoulders to turn together. We can instead keep the shoulders still while the head turns, or turn the shoulders one way and the head the other, which stretches the muscles on the side opposite the turn more strongly. Turning back and forth alternately contracts and lengthens both sides of the neck, bringing circulation, strength, and relaxation. He names habits that feed the problem: lifting the head as we bend forward, raising the shoulders toward the ears under stress, cradling a phone against the shoulder, and pulling the spine with the head when moving into backbends and out of forward bends. On the sequencing page, the regional notes carry the same principle into Triangle, Side Angle, and twists.

Where the evidence stands

What has been tested

Kraftsow’s method has been part of the research on yoga for back pain. His institute reports that he developed the yoga protocol for a National Institutes of Health study of chronic low back pain published in the Annals of Internal Medicine in 2005. A larger trial by Karen Sherman and colleagues, published in 2011, assigned 228 adults with chronic low back pain to Viniyoga-style classes, conventional stretching classes, or a self-care book. After twelve weeks both class groups had better back function and fewer symptoms than the book group, and the gains lasted to six months. Yoga did no better than stretching, which led Sherman to read its benefits as largely physical.

Across the wider field, the National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health summarizes short-term improvements from yoga for chronic low back and neck pain; a 2022 Cochrane review rated the low back gains as small. The emotional-health and chronic-disease chapters rest mostly on case studies and tradition, and I read them as a teacher’s experience, carefully recorded, and as questions still waiting for trials.

Reading a 1999 book in 2026

Some health claims in the book are stronger than current evidence supports. It describes heart disease as the “direct result” of chronic anger and hostility, where research today treats anger as one risk factor among many, and it uses the language of purification and toxins for reducing practices. I keep those as the book’s claims and leave them apart from the measured findings above.

Yoga therapy is also its own professional field now. The International Association of Yoga Therapists accredits training programs and certifies yoga therapists (C-IAYT), and a teacher trained in adaptations works alongside that field and alongside medical care.
