Breath

Prāṇa, the life force, and āyāma, its expansion: a searchable field guide to the breath practices

Find a breath, then read why it works

Long before yoga was identified with posture, it was identified with the breath. As James Mallinson and Mark Singleton put it in Roots of Yoga, their anthology of the primary sources, “Today, the physical practice of yoga is popularly identified with bodily postures, but in pre-modern India it was breath-control that was the defining practice of physical yoga.” The classical Hatha texts make the reason plain. The Hatha Yoga Pradipika ties the breath directly to the mind: “Respiration being disturbed, the mind becomes disturbed. By restraining respiration, the Yogi gets steadiness of mind” (II.2). Patanjali had already set pranayama as the fourth of the eight limbs, defining it in the Yoga Sutras as the pause that follows a steady seat, when the flow of inhalation and exhalation is stilled (2.49). The techniques gathered below are branches of that old root.

The directory

Thirty-seven breathing techniques

Not every breath practice below comes from the same place. Some are named in the classical Hatha Yoga texts (the Hatha Yoga Pradipika and Gheranda Samhita); others belong to the broader living yogic tradition, including modern teaching lineages and programs like Sudarshan Kriya Yoga and the Kundalini Yoga of Yogi Bhajan; and several are Western clinical or physiological techniques, some borrowed from pranayama and re-tested in hospitals and labs, others developed independently. Each card names its source, and the provenance filter below lets you sort by it. Within each card, peer-reviewed studies are listed first and marked in teal; the more accessible teaching sources follow.

Lion’s Breath
Simhasana · Simha Pranayama
EnergizingWarmingRespiratory
Classical Pranayama Text
 ▾

How to practice

  1. Kneel with the hips on the heels, or sit tall in any comfortable seat. Place the palms on the knees and spread the fingers wide.
  2. Inhale fully through the nose.
  3. Open the mouth wide, stretch the tongue out and down toward the chin, and widen the eyes, directing the gaze to the eyebrow centre or the tip of the nose.
  4. Exhale forcefully through the mouth with a clear "haaa" from the back of the throat, like a roar. The exhale is audible and complete.
  5. Close the mouth, breathe normally for a few cycles, and repeat 3 to 6 times.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

The one practice in the directory that asks us to make an undignified noise, which is most of its value. It releases held tension in the jaw, throat, and face, the places we clench without noticing, and it interrupts a self-conscious mood faster than any calming breath. The Gheranda Samhita presents Simhasana as a seat that destroys disease; teachers today use it for the throat and for shaking off staleness. Under the banyan tree it carries Narasimha’s roar: the sound that dissolves what cannot be sustained.

Hand position

Palms on the knees, fingers actively splayed like a lion’s claws. Some traditions cross the wrists or press the palms to the floor between the knees.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Well suited to blocked noses, since the powerful exhale leaves through the mouth. If the nasal inhale is difficult, inhale through the mouth instead and lose nothing.

Precautions

Ease off with jaw pain or TMJ: keep the mouth comfortably open rather than maximally wide. Do not force the eyes wide if they strain, and skip the sound if it hurts the throat.

Spontaneous Retention
Kevala Kumbhaka · Kevali
CalmingFocus
Classical Pranayama Text
 ▾

How to practice

  1. This is the one technique here that cannot be performed on demand. It is approached rather than done.
  2. Practice a long, settled round of any slow breath: Nadi Shodhana, Ujjayi, or Sama Vritti, until the breath is smooth and the count stops mattering.
  3. Let the deliberate control fall away. Stop lengthening, stop counting, and simply let the breath breathe itself.
  4. Notice the natural pause that already lives at the end of each exhale and each inhale. Do not create it. Only stop interrupting it.
  5. The Hatha Yoga Pradipika describes the breath eventually suspending itself, without inhalation or exhalation and without effort. If that happens, it happens; if it does not, the sitting was still complete.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

The Hatha Yoga Pradipika (2.71 to 2.75) treats the eight kumbhakas it names (2.44) as training wheels for this one: retention that arrives on its own, with no closing of nostrils and no counting. The text links it directly to raja yoga. Its practical value is a corrective. It reframes pranayama as something we gradually stop doing, and it makes the goal of the whole limb a state rather than a manoeuvre. The Gheranda Samhita teaches the same effortless retention but frames it differently, calling the one who attains it a knower of yoga and counting Kevali as one of its own eight kumbhakas rather than as the state that lies beyond them.

Hand position

Any settled seat: Chin, Jnana, Bhairava, or Dhyana. Since no nostril is closed, the hands are free to rest.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Among the most accessible practices here, since it requires no nostril control at all. Whichever nostril is dominant today, the practice is only to stop interfering.

Precautions

Never hold the breath to manufacture this. Forced retention is a different practice with real risks; kevala is defined by the absence of force. If we catch ourselves gripping the throat or straining, we return to normal breathing.

Sources
Gheranda Samhita, ch. 5 (Kevali kumbhaka), trans. Mallinson (YogaVidya.com) Hatha Yoga Pradipika, Pancham Sinh translation (Sacred Texts)
Graded Nostril Breathing
Anuloma · Pratiloma · Viloma
CalmingFocusRespiratory
Yogic Tradition
 ▾

How to practice

  1. Anuloma: inhale through both nostrils in Ujjayi, then exhale through one nostril, partially closed by the fingers. Alternate the exhaling side each round. The inhale is free; the exhale is graded.
  2. Pratiloma: the reverse. Inhale through one partially closed nostril, then exhale freely through both in Ujjayi. The inhale is graded; the exhale is free.
  3. Viloma: interrupt the breath itself. Inhale a third, pause two seconds; another third, pause; the last third, pause; then exhale smoothly. Later, interrupt the exhale in the same stepped way.
  4. Begin with 5 to 8 rounds of one variation only. These are graded practices and they are meant to be taken in order, over months rather than in one sitting.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

Iyengar’s Light on Pranayama lays out pranayama as a graded curriculum rather than a set of party tricks, and these are its middle rungs. Partially closing a nostril, rather than fully blocking it, teaches a finer control of airflow than Nadi Shodhana asks for, and the stepped Viloma inhale trains the lungs to fill in sections rather than in one grab. In Anuloma the fingers throttle only the exhale, so the out-breath grows longer than the in-breath; in Pratiloma they throttle only the inhale, so the in-breath is the one that lengthens. Iyengar is strict about the order: master Ujjayi and Viloma before attempting Anuloma, and treat Anuloma and Pratiloma as the foundation for the harder ratio work that follows. The value is precision, the sensitivity that makes the longer practices safe rather than forced.

Hand position

Nasikagra or Vishnu mudra on the breathing hand, but the touch is far lighter than in Nadi Shodhana. The finger narrows the nostril rather than sealing it, so the airflow is throttled, not stopped.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Viloma needs no nostril control at all and is the natural choice if one side is restricted, since it interrupts the breath by timing rather than by finger pressure. For Anuloma and Pratiloma, narrow only the open side and let the restricted side stay free.

Precautions

Skip the pauses during pregnancy, with high blood pressure, or at any sign of breathlessness. Build in stages and stop at the first sign of strain rather than pushing to complete the round.

Sources
Iyengar, Light on Pranayama (the source of Anuloma, Pratiloma, and Viloma as taught here) · publisher
Inner Fire
Tummo · gTum-mo · Chandali
EnergizingWarmingFocus
Tibetan Buddhist
 ▾

How to practice

  1. Sit tall. Inhale deeply through the nose and draw the breath down toward the navel.
  2. Hold gently and take the "vase" shape: draw the lower belly slightly in and up, and press the diaphragm down a little, so the held breath is contained like water in a pot below the navel.
  3. Alongside the breath, visualize a small flame at the navel centre, about the size of a thumb, growing brighter and hotter with each round. The visualization is not decoration here; in the tradition it is half the technique.
  4. Release and exhale slowly. Repeat for short rounds, resting whenever the breath asks.
  5. The forceful version combines the vase breath with vigorous belly bellows. That form belongs with a qualified teacher, not a web page.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

The classic heat practice of Tibetan Buddhism, part of the Six Yogas of Naropa, where warmth is a byproduct rather than the goal: the aim is to melt subtle energy into the central channel for insight. It is also the rare traditional claim to be measured directly. Kozhevnikov and colleagues (2013, PLOS ONE) recorded practitioners in Tibet and Nepal raising core body temperature into the mild fever range and sustaining it, finding that forceful breath produced the heat while visualization sustained it. It is included here as the page’s only Tibetan entry, and because it is a genuine bridge between the breath and the imagination.

Hand position

Traditionally the hands rest in Dhyana mudra in the lap, or on the knees. No nostril work is involved.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Both nostrils are used together, so no side-specific control is needed. If nasal breathing is restricted, take a slower, gentler inhale rather than a forceful one, and lean on the visualization.

Precautions

The forceful form involves vigorous breathing and retention and should be learned in person within a lineage. Avoid retention if pregnant, or with high blood pressure, heart conditions, glaucoma, or epilepsy. This entry describes the practice; it does not teach it.

Reduced Breathing
The Buteyko Method
RespiratoryCalming
Western Medicine
 ▾

How to practice

  1. Sit upright and breathe through the nose only, with the mouth closed. Nasal breathing is the whole foundation of the method.
  2. Measure the control pause: after a normal, quiet exhale, pinch the nose and count the seconds until the first definite urge to breathe. Release and breathe normally. This is a measurement, not a challenge, so never push to the maximum.
  3. For reduced breathing, let each breath become slightly smaller and quieter than usual, aiming for a light air hunger we can hold comfortably for several minutes.
  4. Breathe low and slow into the diaphragm. The goal is less air, not more.
  5. Recheck the control pause after a few minutes of practice.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

Developed in the 1950s by the Ukrainian physician Konstantin Buteyko, this is the directory’s clearest counterexample to the assumption that deeper is better. It proposes that chronic over-breathing lowers carbon dioxide and that gently tolerating a little more of it eases airway constriction. Randomized trials in asthma consistently find reduced symptoms and, notably, reduced reliever medication use; the evidence for changed underlying lung function is much weaker. Worth practicing for the shift it produces in how we breathe all day, and worth citing carefully.

Hand position

Hands rest on the thighs or lightly on the belly and chest so we can feel the movement shrink. The nose is pinched only for the control-pause measurement.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Awkward if nasal breathing is genuinely obstructed, since it is the premise. Clear the nose first with a saline rinse or gentle Bhramari; if one side is chronically blocked, the light-breathing part still works through whichever side is open.

Precautions

Never use this to replace asthma medication, and keep the reliever inhaler to hand. Air hunger should stay mild and comfortable. Avoid breath holds if pregnant, or with heart conditions, uncontrolled hypertension, epilepsy, or diabetes, and, with a respiratory diagnosis, speak to a clinician before starting.

How to practice

  1. Sit tall in a comfortable cross-legged position or chair, spine long and shoulders soft. Rest the left hand on the left knee in Jnana mudra.
  2. Bring the right hand to Vishnu mudra: fold the index and middle fingers into the palm, leaving the thumb, ring, and little fingers free.
  3. Close the right nostril with the thumb. Inhale slowly and fully through the left nostril.
  4. Close the left nostril with the ring finger, release the thumb, and exhale slowly through the right nostril.
  5. Inhale through the right nostril, then close it with the thumb and exhale through the left. This completes one round.
  6. Continue for 5–10 rounds, keeping the breath smooth and unforced.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

The tradition's great balancing breath, evening the flow between the two nadis (ida and pingala) and quieting the mind before meditation. Controlled trials in the systematic review report lower blood pressure and pulse; a 2016 study in Scientific Reports found a single session improved fine-motor and spatial-memory retention, pointing to real gains in attention and cognitive control. In the tantric map these two nadis flank a central channel, the sushumna, and evening them is the classical preparation for drawing prana into that center, which is the ground of kundalini and chakra practice. That thread is not a separate page here; it runs through the Trataka and Kundalini Kriya practices on the meditation page and the chakra work on dharana, with Satyananda’s Kundalini Tantra and Goswami’s Layayoga as the fuller sources.

Hand position

Vishnu mudra on the breathing hand: index and middle fingers folded to the palm; thumb closes the right nostril, ring and little fingers close the left. The opposite hand rests in Jnana mudra on the knee.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

If one side is chronically restricted, let the breath be uneven rather than forced equal, since a slower count on the tighter side is still a complete breath. To open a tight nostril, use the free hand to press the outer wall (ala) gently outward rather than closing it. See the deviated septum section below for the visualization variant and other adjustments.

Precautions

Children under 12 and anyone with a heart condition should practice without holding the breath between the inhale and exhale.

Ocean Breath
Ujjayi · the Victorious Breath
CalmingFocusRespiratoryDigestion
Classical Pranayama Text

How to practice

  1. Sit with a tall spine, mouth closed, breathing only through the nose.
  2. Gently narrow the back of the throat, as if slightly fogging a mirror or about to whisper a secret.
  3. Inhale slowly through the nose, letting the breath brush the narrowed throat and make a soft, oceanic whisper.
  4. Pause briefly at the top of the inhale without straining.
  5. Exhale slowly through the nose with the same gentle throat constriction, longer than the inhale.
  6. Continue for 5–10 minutes, or sustain it through an asana practice.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

The audible throat friction naturally slows and lengthens each breath, exactly the slow-breathing pattern shown to raise vagal (parasympathetic) tone and steady the mind. It gently warms the body, builds concentration, and is the breath most often paired with a moving asana practice to keep attention anchored. The Gheranda Samhita also files it among the digestive breaths, saying it wards off indigestion along with disorders of phlegm and bile, which fits its place as an udana practice at the throat.

The Krishnamacharya lineage makes that pairing stronger than mere accompaniment: the breath leads. In Desikachar’s Heart of Yoga the order of causation is explicit, that “we make sure that the breathing initiates the movement. The length of the breath will determine the speed of the movement,” so breath and asana become one process rather than two.

Hand position

Jnana mudra resting on the knees; no nostril work is required.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Both nostrils stay open throughout, so this is fully accessible regardless of nasal restriction. If nasal breathing is difficult on both sides, the same throat constriction can be practiced with the mouth held slightly open as a bridge technique until the passages clear.

Precautions

Avoid forcing the throat constriction if it causes strain; those with serious cardiac issues or hypertension should practice gently and briefly.

Humming Bee Breath
Bhramari
CalmingSleepFocusBlood Pressure
Classical Pranayama Text

How to practice

  1. Sit comfortably with the eyes closed and the face relaxed.
  2. Raise the hands to the face and gently close the ear flaps (tragus) with the thumbs.
  3. Rest the index fingers lightly on the brow, the middle fingers on the closed eyelids, and let the ring and little fingers rest beside the nose and mouth (Shanmukhi mudra).
  4. Inhale deeply through the nose.
  5. Exhale slowly through the nose while humming like a bee, feeling the vibration in the head and chest.
  6. Repeat for 5–10 rounds.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

The humming both lengthens the exhale and vibrates the sinuses, associated with a marked rise in nasal nitric oxide. Studied for lowering blood pressure and heart rate and calming the nervous system, it is a reliable pre-sleep and anti-anxiety practice that quiets mental chatter and draws the senses inward (pratyahara). One note on lineage: the audible outward hum taught here follows the Hatha Yoga Pradipika. The Gheranda Samhita’s Bhramari kumbhaka (ch. 5) is a different, silent practice, done in a quiet place with the ears closed, listening for nada (the inner unstruck sound) that it says leads toward samadhi.

Hand position

Shanmukhi mudra, "closing the seven gates": thumbs seal the ears, index fingers on the brow, middle fingers on closed eyes, ring fingers beside the nostrils, little fingers at the corners of the mouth.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

No nostril closure is required, so this technique is fully accessible regardless of nasal restriction. If pressing the ears feels uncomfortable, resting the fingertips near the ear canal without fully sealing it still carries much of the calming effect.

Precautions

Generally considered safe for most practitioners, with no significant precautions reported.

Bellows Breath
Bhastrika
EnergizingWarmingRespiratoryDigestion
Classical Pranayama Text

How to practice

  1. Sit in a comfortable meditative posture with the spine straight and eyes closed.
  2. Inhale deeply through both nostrils, then exhale forcefully through the nose, pumping the navel toward the spine.
  3. Let the next inhale follow immediately with equal force; keep inhale and exhale equally active and rhythmic.
  4. Perform 10–20 forceful breaths, then take one deep inhale, hold briefly, and release slowly.
  5. Rest with normal breathing before repeating for 2–3 rounds.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

Named for the blacksmith's bellows, both inhale and exhale are forceful and equal. It raises metabolic heat and alertness, directly strengthens the inspiratory and expiratory muscles, and is used to shake off sluggishness and generate warmth. A classic morning or pre-practice energizer. This is the one forceful practice with a classical digestive claim behind it: the Hatha Yoga Pradipika says it destroys vata, pitta, and phlegm and increases the gastric fire, which in the language of the five vayus is samana kindled at the navel. That claim has not been tested in a trial, as the digestion section sets out.

Hand position

Jnana mudra on the knees; beginners sometimes rest the hands lightly on the belly to feel the pumping rhythm.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Because both nostrils stay open, a deviated septum is rarely a barrier here. Slow the pace or shorten the rounds if congestion makes forceful nasal breathing uncomfortable.

Precautions

Avoid with heart conditions, hypertension, pregnancy, vertigo, recent abdominal surgery, spinal conditions, glaucoma, or detached retina. Build up gradually and stop if lightheaded.

Skull-Shining Breath
Kapalabhati
EnergizingDetoxRespiratoryDigestion
Classical Text (Shatkarma)

How to practice

  1. Sit tall with the hands resting on the knees, palms down. Take a few natural breaths to settle.
  2. Exhale sharply through the nose by pulling the navel in toward the spine; let the inhale that follows happen passively and naturally.
  3. Continue short, active exhales with passive inhales, keeping the chest still, for one round of 20–30 breaths.
  4. Rest with normal breathing, then repeat for 2–3 rounds, building endurance gradually.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

Classed as a cleansing kriya rather than a pure pranayama, "skull-shining breath" is named for the bright, clear feeling it leaves in the head. The rapid active exhales tone the abdominal muscles, clear the respiratory passages, and are studied for improving pulmonary function. Deeply energizing and often used to clear mental fog. In the language of the five vayus the navel pumping works samana at the centre and drives apana downward, which is why modern teachers file it under digestion. Worth knowing: the Hatha Yoga Pradipika assigns Kapalabhati to drying up disorders of phlegm, so the digestive claim here is a modern one, and no trial has measured a digestive outcome for it. See the digestion section.

Hand position

Jnana mudra on the knees, or hands lightly on the belly for beginners learning to feel the pumping action.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Both nostrils stay open, so nasal restriction is rarely a barrier; reduce speed and force if the nose feels irritated or congestion makes the sharp exhale uncomfortable.

Precautions

Avoid during pregnancy or menstruation, and with high or low blood pressure, heart disease, hernia, gastric ulcer, epilepsy, vertigo, migraine, significant nosebleeds, detached retina, glaucoma, stroke history, or recent abdominal surgery. A published case report links forceful practice to a pneumothorax, so build up gradually and stop with any chest discomfort. Physicians who work with breath-based therapies add a neuropsychiatric caution: people with respiratory problems, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress, bipolar disorder, uncontrolled hypertension, or seizure disorders "can be triggered to have respiratory distress, panic attacks, dissociative episodes, manic episodes, increased blood pressure, or a seizure" by rapid or forceful breathing, which acts as a form of hyperventilation (Gerbarg and Brown).

Cooling Breath
Sheetali

Classical Pranayama Text

How to practice

  1. Sit comfortably and take a few natural breaths.
  2. Curl the tongue lengthwise into a tube and extend it slightly past the lips. (If the tongue won't curl, a genetic trait not everyone has, use Sitkari instead.)
  3. Inhale slowly through the tongue's tube, feeling the cool air.
  4. Hold briefly, then draw the tongue in, close the mouth, and exhale slowly through the nose.
  5. Repeat for 5–10 rounds.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

Air drawn across the wet, curled tongue arrives cooled, which is why the tradition prescribes it for excess heat, thirst, and irritability. It calms the nervous system and is a natural choice on hot days, after vigorous practice, or whenever the body or temper runs hot. The Gheranda Samhita, which lists Shitali among its eight kumbhakas, frames it as much a digestive breath as a cooling one, saying regular practice wards off indigestion and disorders of kapha and pitta. In vayu language it cools a hot samana, which is the direction to take when digestion runs sharp and acid rather than sluggish.

Hand position

Jnana mudra on the knees.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

The inhale is through the mouth, so this works regardless of nostril restriction; if one nostril is blocked, the exhale can be released through the open side alone, or through the mouth instead of the nose.

Precautions

Best avoided in cold weather or with an active cold, cough, congestion, asthma flare, or chronic constipation.

How to practice

  1. Sit comfortably, open the lips in a slight smile, and bring the upper and lower teeth lightly together.
  2. Inhale slowly through the gaps between the teeth, making a soft hissing sound.
  3. Hold briefly, then close the mouth and exhale slowly through the nose.
  4. Repeat for 5–10 rounds.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

The through-the-teeth cousin of Sheetali, for those who can't roll the tongue. It delivers the same cooling, calming effect, easing heat, thirst, and agitation, and is often chosen for its accessibility.

Hand position

Jnana mudra on the knees.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Like Sheetali, the inhale is through the mouth, making this a reliable substitute for anyone who cannot fully close one nostril, or who cannot curl the tongue for Sheetali.

Precautions

Same cautions as Sheetali: avoid in cold weather, with respiratory congestion, or with chronic constipation.

Right-Nostril Solar Breath
Surya Bhedana
EnergizingWarmingDigestion
Classical Pranayama Text
 ▾

How to practice

  1. Sit comfortably and bring the right hand to Vishnu mudra.
  2. Close the left nostril with the ring finger.
  3. Inhale slowly and fully through the right nostril only.
  4. Close the right nostril with the thumb, release the left, and exhale slowly through the left nostril.
  5. This completes one round; repeat for 5–10 rounds.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

Right-nostril breathing is traditionally linked to the "solar," heating pingala channel and to greater sympathetic activation and alertness. Used to raise energy, warmth, and drive, a morning or pre-activity breath rather than an evening one. The Gheranda Samhita, which counts Surya Bhedana among its eight kumbhakas, says it increases the bodily fire, matching the warming intent. Science of Breath places this in svara yoga, the yogic study of the breath’s alternation: the right-nostril channel is pingala or surya, and the yogis observed that breathing through the right side inclines a person toward activity, alertness, and the outward, warming processes of the body, digestion among them, so students of svara yoga opened the right nostril before eating. That is a traditional claim and no trial has tested it; see the digestion section.

Hand position

Vishnu mudra, as in Nadi Shodhana, breathing only in the right-in, left-out direction.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

If the right nostril is the restricted side, use the free hand to gently draw the ala outward and widen it, or substitute Bhastrika or Kapalabhati for a similarly warming effect that doesn't require single-nostril closure. See the deviated septum section below for more.

Precautions

Avoid with heart disease, high blood pressure, epilepsy, or fever, and after recent brain, heart, or abdominal surgery, since it raises internal heat.

  1. Sit comfortably and bring the right hand to Vishnu mudra.

  3. Inhale slowly and fully through the left nostril only.
  4. Close the left nostril with the ring finger, release the thumb, and exhale slowly through the right nostril.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

The mirror of Surya Bhedana. Left-nostril breathing is linked to the "lunar," cooling ida channel and to parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) activation. Used to cool, calm, and quiet the system, often in the evening or to ease agitation; the left-nostril and vagal emphasis is also associated with lowering blood pressure. Science of Breath gives the mirror in svara yoga: the left-nostril channel is ida or chandra, and breathing through the left side inclines a person toward a quieter, more passive, inward-oriented state, so more passive intakes such as drinking water were traditionally taken with the left nostril open.

Hand position

Vishnu mudra, breathing in the left-in, right-out direction.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

If the left nostril is restricted, substitute Sheetali or Sitkari for a comparable cooling effect that inhales through the mouth instead. See the deviated septum section below for more adjustments.

Precautions

Avoid in heavy winter cold, with an active cold or congestion, or with low blood pressure or epilepsy.

How to practice

  1. Sit or lie down comfortably, one hand on the belly and one on the chest.
  2. Inhale slowly and let the breath fill first the belly (feel the lower hand rise), then the lower ribs, then the upper chest: three parts of one continuous inhale.
  3. Exhale in reverse: release from the chest, then the ribs, then draw the navel gently in to empty the belly.
  4. Keep the transitions smooth so the breath feels like a single rising and falling wave.
  5. Continue for 5–10 minutes.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

The foundational breath of yoga: it teaches full use of the diaphragm and the whole breathing apparatus, which most people underuse. Calming and grounding, it builds breath awareness and lung capacity and is the base on which the more advanced pranayamas are built. Rama, Ballentine, and Hymes single out diaphragmatic breathing as the most efficient of the three phases the complete breath combines (diaphragmatic, then thoracic, then clavicular), since the lower lungs hold the most blood; shallow chest breathing does more work for less oxygen. They go further, noting that the reverse habit, chronic chest breathing, has been proposed to perpetuate a state of sympathetic (fight-or-flight) arousal, which reframes the complete breath as much a way of settling the nervous system as of moving air. It is also the closest yogic relative of the abdominal breathing used in the reflux trials in the digestion section, since both begin at the belly and slow the whole breath.

Hand position

No mudra required; the hands rest on the belly and chest to feel the three-part movement. Later they can rest in Jnana mudra on the knees.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Both nostrils are used together, so nasal restriction is not a barrier; if both sides are congested, the same three-part wave can be practiced with a soft mouth-breath until the nose clears.

Precautions

Very safe. Simply avoid over-filling to the point of strain; the breath should stay smooth and comfortable.

How to practice

  1. Stand with the feet hip width and the knees softly bent, hands on the thighs, or sit forward on a chair. Practise on an empty stomach.
  2. Exhale fully through the mouth and hold the breath out.
  3. With the breath still out, draw the belly in toward the spine and release it, repeating the pump in an easy rhythm for as long as the retention stays comfortable.
  4. Release the belly, lift the head, and inhale slowly. Breathe normally for several breaths.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

This is the practice the tradition aims most directly at digestion. The Gheranda Samhita describes drawing the navel back toward the spine a hundred times and says that by it the diseases of the stomach are cured and the gastric fire becomes intense. In the language of the five vayus it works samana at the navel and frees apana below. One small study by Minvaleev and colleagues measured blood flow in the superior mesenteric artery before and after a hundred repetitions in twelve healthy volunteers and found it increased, which is a surrogate for circulation to the gut rather than a measure of digestion itself, in a study with no control group. Hand position

Hands rest on the thighs while standing, which lets the belly move freely.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Both nostrils stay open and the retention is after the exhale, so nasal restriction is rarely a barrier. The inhale afterwards can be taken through whichever side is open.

Practise on an empty stomach, at least two to three hours after eating. How to practice

  1. Learn Uddiyana Bandha first. Nauli begins from the same hollow.
  2. Standing, feet apart, knees bent, hands on the thighs, exhale fully and hold the breath out.
  3. Draw the belly up and back under the ribs, then press the hands down on the thighs so the central column of the abdomen stands forward. That isolation is madhyama nauli.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

Nauli carries the clearest digestive claim in the classical literature. It is a shatkarma, a cleansing practice, rather than a pranayama, and it asks for a teacher: the isolation is difficult to find from a written description and easy to strain for. No trial has measured a digestive outcome for it.

Hand position

Hands press down on the thighs, which is what lets the abdominal wall lift and the column separate.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Breath is held out, so nasal restriction makes no difference here.

Precautions

Practise on an empty stomach. Teaching sources advise against it in pregnancy and menstruation, with kidney or gallstones, with hernia, ulcers, heart disease, high blood pressure, or after recent abdominal surgery. These cautions are traditional rather than researched. Learn it from a teacher.

Why practice it

How to practice

  1. Sit tall with the spine erect and hands resting on the knees.
  2. Inhale fully, then exhale completely through the nose, emptying the lungs.
  3. Hold the breath out (external retention). Traditionally this is combined with three locks: draw the chin toward the chest (jalandhara bandha), lift the pelvic floor (mula bandha), and draw the navel up and back toward the spine (uddiyana bandha).

Why practice it

An advanced retention practice. Hand position

Jnana mudra on the knees; the "hand positions" that matter here are the three bandhas (throat, belly, pelvic floor) rather than a finger mudra.

Both nostrils are used and the challenge is the breath-out hold, not airflow, so nasal restriction is not a barrier. Shorten the retention if congestion makes the following inhale feel urgent.

How to practice

  1. Sit tall and comfortable with the eyes closed and the face soft.
  2. Inhale slowly and fully through the nose.
  3. On the exhale, chant "OM" (A-U-M), letting the sound stretch across the whole out-breath: the open "aaah," the rounding "ooo," and the humming "mmm" that lingers.
  4. Feel the vibration move through the chest, throat, and head. Let each inhale arrive naturally before the next chant.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

Chanting naturally stretches the exhale and slows breathing toward the ~6-breaths-per-minute range. Deeply centering, it prepares the mind for meditation and eases the way toward sleep.

Hand position

Jnana or Chin mudra resting on the knees; no nostril work required.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

The inhale can be nasal or, if both sides are congested, a soft mouth-breath; the chant itself is on the exhale, so nasal restriction is not a barrier.

Precautions

Very safe. Simply let the breath stay comfortable and don't force the length of the chant.

How to practice

  3. Breathe in gently through the nose, counting steadily to five if we can, and feel the belly (not the chest) rise under the lower hand.

How to practice

  1. Sit or lie comfortably and breathe through the nose.
  2. Inhale smoothly for a count of about five or six seconds.
  3. Exhale just as smoothly for the same five or six seconds, with no pause or hold at either end.
  4. This gives roughly five to six breaths per minute, the "resonance" rate at which heart rate and breath synchronize.
  5. Continue for 10–20 minutes; many people use a visual or audio pacer to hold the rhythm.

Benefit & purpose

Why practice it

Slowing to around six breaths per minute maximizes heart-rate variability and strengthens the baroreflex, the system that regulates blood pressure. Well studied for lowering blood pressure, easing stress and anxiety, and producing a balanced, coherent physiological state. It is essentially the Western, instrument-measured formalization of the slow breathing the yogic tradition arrived at through chanting and Ujjayi.

Hand position

No mudra; hands rest wherever comfortable. The focus is entirely on the even five-to-six-second pace.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

Both nostrils are used together and the breath is slow and soft, so nasal restriction is rarely a barrier; a gentle mouth-breath can be used if both sides are congested.

Precautions

Very safe. If the slow pace feels like air hunger at first, lengthen the count gradually over several sessions.

How to practice

  1. Sit or lie comfortably and breathe normally to begin.
  2. Inhale slowly through the nose until the lungs feel about full.
  3. Without exhaling, take a second, short, sharp sip of air through the nose to top the lungs off completely (the "double inhale").

No mudra; hands rest comfortably. The defining feature is the double-inhale followed by an extended exhale.

Adaptation for restricted or uneven nostril breathing

If a nostril is blocked, the double inhale can be taken through the mouth; the extended exhale is already through the mouth, so nasal restriction is not a barrier.

Precautions

Very safe. Simply keep the double inhale gentle rather than gasping.

How to practice

  1. Sit tall with a long spine, hands resting on the knees, eyes lightly closed.
  2. Breathe through the nose with a rapid, continuous, rhythmic breath in which the inhale and exhale are equal, with no pause between them.

Avoid during pregnancy and menstruation, and with heart disease, high blood pressure, vertigo, epilepsy, hernia, gastric ulcer, or recent abdominal surgery. Build up slowly and stop if lightheaded.

What the breath is, and why it carries
What pranayama is

The deliberate shaping of breath

Pranayama joins two Sanskrit roots: prana, the breath of life, and ayama, expansion or regulation. It is the deliberate modification of breathing, ranging from rapid diaphragmatic breathing to slow, deep breathing, alternate-nostril breathing, and breath retention, most often practiced seated. The tradition names four parts to every round: puraka (inhalation), recaka (exhalation), antah kumbhaka (breath held in), and bahih kumbhaka (breath held out). In Patanjali's Yoga Sutras, pranayama is the fourth limb of the eight-fold path. The reciprocity is the practical basis of the whole practice: a change of state alters the breath on its own, and the relationship also runs the other way, so that, as the teacher Jennifer Cohen Harper puts it in plain terms, “when we change our breath, we can change our energetic state, encouraging increased energy or an increased sense of calm.” That is accessible framing rather than textual authority; the primary sources on prana lead, and this only restates why they matter.

The classical link between breath and mind is stated plainly in the Hatha Yoga Pradipika, whose second chapter opens on pranayama. In B.K.S. Iyengar’s rendering: “When the breath is irregular, the mind wavers; when the breath is steady, so is the mind. To attain steadiness, the yogi should restrain his breath.” Iyengar reads the held breath, kumbhaka, as the heart of the practice: “when the breath is stilled in kumbhaka, the senses are stilled and the mind becomes silent. Breath is the bridge between the body, the senses and the mind.” The word itself is drawn from an image, since “kumbha means a pot which can be full or empty,” and kumbhaka “is the art of retaining the breath in a state of suspense,” whether the pause falls after an inhalation or after an exhalation. The wording here is Iyengar’s own, in a practitioner manual rather than a scholarly edition of the Hatha texts.

Modern anatomy adds a caution that runs the other way. The anatomist H. David Coulter names a stress pattern he calls paradoxical breathing, in which the belly is pulled in on the inhale and pushed out on the exhale, the reverse of relaxed abdominal breathing. It is the breath of shock and fright, and in his account “paradoxical breathing stimulates the sympathetic nervous system even more than thoracic breathing.” The point is a useful mirror to the practice: where this pattern winds the nervous system up, the slow, full, belly-led breath of pranayama is one of the plainest ways to settle it back down. This is Coulter’s reading in a practitioner-anatomy manual rather than a peer-reviewed physiology finding.

Patanjali says plainly what the practice is for: pranayama thins the covering over the inner light and makes the mind fit for concentration (Yoga Sutra 2.52 to 2.53). B.K.S. Iyengar cautions in Light on Life that this is not the same as deep breathing. Breath, he writes, is the vehicle of consciousness, and by its slow, measured observation we learn to tug attention away from vasana (external desire) toward prajna (a clear, intelligent awareness): as the breath stills the mind, the senses unhook and bend inward. He is emphatic that it cannot be forced. Pranayama is not performed by the power of will; the breath must be enticed, he says, the way one catches a horse in a field, not by chasing it but by standing still with an apple in one’s hand. Receptivity, not effort, is what the practice asks, which is why the technique cards below all favor building slowly over pushing to complete a round.

A 2020 systematic review in the International Journal of Yoga gathered eighteen controlled trials on pranayama alone and found real, measurable effects: improved pulse rate, blood pressure, and lung function in bronchial asthma; better symptom and activity scores in COPD; reduced fatigue and anxiety in cancer patients; and lower blood pressure in hypertension. The proposed mechanisms are physiological and specific: slow, deep breathing stretches lung tissue in a way that shifts the autonomic nervous system from sympathetic toward parasympathetic dominance, largely through the vagus nerve, while forceful practices like Bhastrika and Kapalabhati train the inspiratory and expiratory muscles directly.

Before we begin

Practice in a well-ventilated room, seated with an erect spine, on a fairly empty stomach. Keep the face and shoulders relaxed. Beginners need not start seated at all. In the Iyengar-lineage method Richard Rosen sets out, pranayama is first practiced lying supine, the torso supported on a folded blanket so the floor holds the body while the breath is learned; “You can probably expect to recline for at least the first four months of practice,” he writes, before a sitting posture adds its own demand. There is a physiological reason the floor is the easiest teacher. Lying down gives, as David Coulter puts it, “the simplest possible method of breathing: the diaphragm is active in both inhalation and exhalation, the intercostal muscles act only to keep the chest stable, and the abdominal muscles remain completely relaxed.” Every technique above can be built slowly: fewer rounds, gentler force, shorter holds, always. If anything produces dizziness, breathlessness, or strain, return to normal breathing and ease off.

Inhale Exhale
Ujjayi · the breath that carries

Breath as waves, the exhale as the crash

Before the mind can travel anywhere, the breath must become an ocean. The breath that carries meditation in this tradition is Ujjayi, often translated as the victorious breath, and just as often called the ocean breath, because that is exactly what it sounds like.

To breathe Ujjayi, we narrow the passage at the back of the throat, gently bringing the vocal folds toward one another so the air must whisper as it passes. The mouth stays closed; the breath moves only through the nose. The narrowing turns silent air into sound, a soft oceanic hush that rises on the inhale and falls on the exhale. The Sanskrit ujjayi joins ut, meaning upward or expanding, with jaya, meaning victory: the breath that expands and conquers through steadiness.

Listen to the surf and the rhythm is already familiar. The inhale is the wave gathering itself, drawing up and back, swelling as it rises toward the shore. It builds. It lifts. And then the exhale is the crash: the long, surrendered fall of water onto sand, the wave giving everything it gathered back to the beach. The inhale collects the world. The exhale lets it go. A meditation is nothing more than ten thousand of these waves, each one a small rehearsal of holding on and letting go.

This is why the exhale is where the practice deepens. A wave that only rises is a wave that never breaks. The crash, the release, the slight emptiness at the bottom of the breath before the next swell gathers, is where the nervous system softens and the mind loosens its grip. Lengthen the exhale and the waters grow calm. When the surface of the breath is calm, the depths beneath it become visible.

Practice · the ocean breath
Riding the wave
  1. Sit so the spine is tall and the shoulders are soft. Close the mouth. Let the breath move only through the nose.
  2. On the inhale, imagine a wave gathering. Let it rise from the belly to the chest to the throat, slightly narrowing the back of the throat so the breath makes a soft oceanic sound, like wind through a shell.
  3. At the top of the inhale, the wave is full and poised, about to break. Do not rush it.
  4. On the exhale, let the wave crash: a slow, complete release through that same whispering throat, longer than the inhale, until the water has fully returned to the sea.
  5. Rest for a heartbeat in the empty trough at the bottom. Then let the next wave gather on its own. Continue for ten breaths, then twenty, listening to the tide we are making.

Hand positions & seals

The mudras, and when to use each

A mudra is a seal: a gesture that closes a circuit so that attention, breath, or energy stops leaking outward. Some are purely practical, a way to reach the nostrils without craning the wrist. Others are subtle, and the tradition treats them as directing prana rather than merely holding a shape. Below is what each one is, how to form it, and the situation it belongs in. The technique cards above name the mudra each practice uses.

Chin Mudra
chin mudra · the seal of consciousness

Form it: tip of the thumb and tip of the index finger touch, making a soft circle. The other three fingers stay long and easy. Hands rest on the knees or thighs with the palms facing down.

When to use Grounding and settling. Palms down turns the practice earthward, so this is the one for anxious or scattered days, for long seated breathing, and for anyone who feels floaty. The most common seat for Dirga and Sama Vritti.
Jnana Mudra
jnana mudra · the seal of knowledge

Form it: identical to Chin, thumb and index touching, three fingers long, but the hands rest on the knees with the palms facing up.

When to use Receptivity and meditation. Palms up opens the practice skyward and is the usual seat for silent sitting, mantra, and self-inquiry. The only difference from Chin is the direction the palm faces, and the tradition considers that difference the whole point.
Chinmaya Mudra
chinmaya mudra · the seal of awareness

Form it: thumb and index touch as in Chin, and the remaining three fingers curl into the palm. Hands on the thighs, palms up.

When to use The middle step of the classic pranayama sequence Chin, Chinmaya, Adi, Brahma. Practitioners report the breath moving into the mid-chest here. Use it when we want the ribs to do the work.
Adi Mudra
adi mudra · the first seal

Form it: place the thumb at the base of the little finger, then fold the other four fingers over it into a soft fist. Rest the fists on the thighs, palms down.

When to use Calming, and to deepen diaphragmatic breath. This is the infant’s natural fist, and it tends to draw the breath low. Good before sleep, in Yoga Nidra, and at the start of a session when the breath is still high and shallow.
Brahma Mudra
brahma mudra · the creator’s seal

Form it: make Adi Mudra in both hands, then press the two fists together at the knuckles and rest them against the navel, palms turned up.

When to use Full yogic breath. It is the last step of the Chin, Chinmaya, Adi, Brahma sequence, and the one for the complete three-part inhale when we want belly, ribs, and collarbones all working.
Vishnu Mudra
vishnu mudra · the nostril seal

Form it: in the right hand, fold the index and middle fingers fully into the palm. The thumb stays free to close the right nostril; the ring and little fingers stay free to close the left. The wrist stays soft and the elbow drops.

When to use Every nostril technique: Nadi Shodhana, Surya Bhedana, Chandra Bhedana. If the shoulder tires on long rounds, switch to Nasikagra rather than shrugging.
Nasikagra Mudra
nasikagra / nasagra mudra · the nose-tip seal

Form it: rest the index and middle fingers lightly at the eyebrow centre instead of folding them away. Thumb closes the right nostril, ring finger the left, exactly as in Vishnu.

When to use The same nostril work as Vishnu, but easier on the hand and it parks two fingers at the third eye, which many find steadying. The better choice for long alternate-nostril sessions and for anyone with finger or wrist pain.
Shanmukhi Mudra
shanmukhi mudra · closing the seven gates

Form it: both hands come to the face. Thumbs seal the ears, index fingers rest lightly on the closed eyelids or brow, middle fingers beside the nostrils, ring fingers just above the lips, little fingers just below. Elbows lift out to the sides. The fingers rest; they never press the eyeballs.

When to use Bhramari, and pratyahara generally. Sealing the senses makes the humming resonate inside the skull and pulls attention off the outer world. Ease the pressure at once if there is any strain in the eyes.
Kaki Mudra
kaki mudra · the crow’s beak

Form it: purse the lips into a narrow O, like a beak, and draw the breath in through that small opening. The tongue stays flat.

When to use Cooling. This is the fallback for Sheetali when the tongue will not roll, which is partly genetic and not a failing. It delivers the same cooled inhale across the wet surfaces of the mouth.
Bhairava & Bhairavi Mudra
bhairava mudra · the fierce seal

Form it: rest both hands in the lap, palms up, one hand cradled inside the other. Right hand on top of left is Bhairava; left on top of right is Bhairavi.

When to use Long sitting, and any time the arms tire of reaching for the knees. It is the least effortful seal in the list, which is exactly why it suits extended kumbhaka and meditation.
Dhyana Mudra
dhyana mudra · the seal of meditation

Form it: hands in the lap, palms up, right resting in left, with the thumb tips lightly touching to form an oval. The thumbs meeting is what distinguishes it from Bhairava.

When to use Seated meditation across Buddhist and yogic traditions. The touching thumbs are a quiet feedback loop: when the mind wanders, the thumbs usually either press together or fall apart, and noticing that is a free attention bell.
Anjali Mudra
anjali mudra · the salutation seal

Form it: palms together at the heart, thumbs lightly touching the sternum, a small hollow left between the palms, shoulders down.

When to use Opening and closing a practice, and any moment of intention or reverence. It is the gesture Arjuna makes before the universal form in Gita 11.14, which is why most classes begin and end inside it.
Prana Mudra
prana mudra · the seal of life force

Form it: bring the tips of the ring finger and little finger to meet the tip of the thumb. Index and middle fingers stay extended. Hands on the knees, palms up.

When to use Fatigue and low energy, traditionally at the start of practice or paired with energizing breath. The tradition frames it as awakening dormant prana vayu; treat that as a claim of the tradition rather than a finding of physiology.
Apana Mudra
apana mudra · the seal of downward flow

Form it: tips of the middle and ring fingers touch the tip of the thumb. Index and little fingers stay long. Palms up on the thighs.

When to use Grounding and release, the counterpart to Prana Mudra. Associated with apana vayu, the downward-moving current governing elimination and letting go. Used when practice needs to settle rather than lift.
Shambhavi Mudra
shambhavi mudra · the eyebrow-centre gaze

Form it: not a hand seal. With the head still, lift the gaze toward the space between the eyebrows, then let the lids half close. Release the instant the eyes ache.

When to use Concentration work, and often paired with Bhramari or mantra. It belongs with the practices on the Dharana page. Keep the sessions short; the eyes are muscles and they fatigue.
Apana Vayu Mudra
apana vayu mudra · mrita sanjivani mudra, the seal of the heart

Form it: fold the index finger in so its tip presses the base of the thumb. Bring the tips of the middle and ring fingers to the tip of the thumb. The little finger stays extended. Both hands rest palms up on the knees.

When to use It combines the hand shapes associated with vayu and apana, and the tradition gives it for palpitations, tightness in the chest, and the gas and bloating that can mimic cardiac symptoms. Its popular name comes from the reviving herb in the Ramayana. Sources for the hand position are modern practitioner ones rather than classical, and this is a seal to sit with, never a reason to wait out chest pain. Chest pain, pressure, or pain radiating into the arm or jaw is an emergency.
Pushan Mudra
pushan mudra · the seal of nourishment

Form it: the two hands differ. For the upper digestive tract, the right hand brings the index and middle fingertips to the thumb tip with the ring and little fingers extended; for the lower tract, the right hand brings the ring and little fingertips to the thumb with the index and middle extended. The left hand holds the same shape throughout: middle and ring fingertips to the thumb tip, index and little extended. Palms up on the thighs.

When to use This is the hand seal the tradition actually files under digestion, and Beth Spindler, writing for Yoga International, describes it as working with prana vayu on one hand and samana vayu on the other, the intake current and the assimilating one. It is given for nausea, wind, and the aftermath of a heavy meal. Treat it as a traditional practice with no trial behind it.
Udana, Samana and Vyana Mudras
a note on three seals that are often listed

Form them: sources disagree, which is the honest answer. For Udana mudra, the Yoga Education Institute gives the ring, middle, and little fingertips to the thumb with the index extended, while other practitioner lists give the index and middle to the thumb instead. Samana mudra is usually all four fingertips gathered at the thumb. Vyana mudra is given as index and middle to the thumb in one list and as index alone in another.

When to use I have left these without instructions on purpose. Prana and Apana mudra above are repeated consistently across modern sources, and these three are not: three sources give three different hand positions, and I could find no classical or Bihar School basis for any of them. If your teacher gives you one, that lineage is a better authority than a contradictory list. The five currents themselves are old and well attested, which is the section below.
Practicing with a deviated septum or nasal restriction

When one side doesn't open the way the other does

Many people breathe more easily through one nostril than the other, whether from a deviated septum, chronic congestion, or the nasal cycle everyone experiences (the natural alternation, which Science of Breath puts at roughly one and three-quarter to two hours, in which one nostril is dominant while the other rests). Nostril-specific pranayama can still be practiced with a few adjustments.

Adaptations
  • Open, don't force. Instead of pressing a finger fully across the blocked nostril, use the opposite hand's finger to gently press against the outer wall (the ala) of the restricted nostril, widening the airway rather than closing it further.
  • Let the ratio be uneven. If air moves more slowly on one side, allow that side a longer count rather than straining to match the open side. The point is a full, unforced breath, not a symmetrical clock.
  • Try the visualization variant. When one nostril cannot be closed or opened at will, breathe naturally through both and simply direct attention to the left side, then the right, in the same rhythm the physical practice would use. Several teachers and clinics describe this mental alternate-nostril breath as carrying much of the calming, focusing effect even without full physical closure.
  • Warm the passage first. A warm shower, a few rounds of gentle Ujjayi, or a saline rinse beforehand can temporarily ease congestion enough to practice normally.
  • Change sides by lying down. Rest on one side for a few minutes and the upper nostril tends to open while the lower one closes. Science of Breath notes this comes not from gravity but from a reflex triggered by the pressure of the down arm against the ribs. To free a blocked left nostril, lie on the right side, and the reverse to free the right. The old manuals use the same trick deliberately: right nostril open (lying on the left) to warm the body and aid digestion, left nostril open (lying on the right) to calm and prepare for sleep.
  • Swap in a two-nostril technique. Ujjayi, Bhastrika, Kapalabhati, Dirga, Viloma, Sama Vritti, and Bahya all breathe through both nostrils at once and require no single-nostril closure, making them reliable substitutes for Nadi Shodhana, Surya Bhedana, or Chandra Bhedana on days when one side won't cooperate.
  • Use a mouth-inhale technique for cooling work. Sheetali and Sitkari inhale through the mouth, so they deliver a cooling breath even when nasal breathing is difficult on both sides.

A structural deviated septum that causes significant, chronic obstruction is worth mentioning to an ENT or physician, particularly before attempting forceful practices like Bhastrika or Kapalabhati on the restricted side.

The five vayusWhere the tradition says the breath goes
Pancha prana

One breath, five directions

Classical yoga and Ayurveda describe prana as one life force moving in five currents, the vayus, each with its own seat and its own direction of travel. The Prashna Upanishad is the usual textual home for them: it places apana in the two lower apertures, prana in the eye, ear, speech, and nose, and says that in the middle is samana, which Shankara glosses as located in the navel and distributing food and drink equally. Charaka gives the same five in medical terms. This is a map of function rather than anatomy, and it is the vocabulary underneath most of the practices on this page.

Prana vayu · the inward and upward current

Seat in the chest and head. It governs taking in: breath, food, sensation, impression. The practices that work with it are the ones that expand the inhale, so Dirga, Ujjayi, Bhastrika, and retention after the inhale.

Apana vayu · the downward and outward current

Seat in the pelvis. Charaka places it in the rectum and gives it the downward movement of urine, faeces, menstrual flow, and birth. It governs elimination and letting go, and it is the current the tradition addresses with long exhales, retention after the exhale, Mula Bandha, squats, and forward folds.

Samana vayu · the assimilating current

Seat at the navel, between prana and apana. This is the digestive one. Shankara describes it as distributing food and drink equally, and the Ayurvedic texts have it stimulating the digestive fire, agni, and moving waste onward. Practices aimed at it work the navel centre: Agnisar Kriya, Nauli, Uddiyana Bandha, Bhastrika, and Bahya Kumbhaka.

Udana vayu · the ascending current

Seat in the throat. It governs speech, song, effort, and upward movement, and the Prashna Upanishad gives it the ascent through sushumna. The practices are the sounding ones: Om chanting, Bhramari, Simhasana, Ujjayi with its throat friction, and Jalandhara Bandha at the throat.

Vyana vayu · the pervading current

No single seat; it moves through the whole body from the heart outward, carrying circulation in the broadest sense. It is the current behind the even, whole-body practices: Sama Vritti, Nadi Shodhana, coherent breathing, and the spread of warmth after a round of anything vigorous.

The vayus and digestion

Two of the five carry the digestive claim. Samana gathers and assimilates at the navel, and apana moves the residue down and out, so a practice for digestion in this language is one that kindles the middle and frees the downward current. That is the reasoning behind Agnisar Kriya, Nauli, Uddiyana Bandha, the navel pumping of Bhastrika and Kapalabhati, and the long exhale of Bahya Kumbhaka.

The classical texts are more specific than the modern summary. The Hatha Yoga Pradipika gives the digestive claim to Nauli, which it says removes dyspepsia and increases appetite and digestion, and to Bhastrika, which it says destroys vata, pitta, and phlegm and increases the gastric fire. It assigns Kapalabhati to drying up disorders of phlegm, so the familiar claim that Kapalabhati aids digestion is a modern one. None of these has been tested for a digestive outcome in a trial I can find, which is the subject of the next section.

Breath and the gut

The one digestive claim with trials behind it

The breath techniques on this page are filed under Digestion because the tradition files them there, through samana and apana, the two currents of the five vayus that carry the claim. One of those claims has been tested in a clinic, and the rest are still the tradition speaking. I keep the two apart here. The Digestion filter in the breath directory now holds twelve practices: the forceful navel work of Agnisar Kriya, Nauli, Uddiyana Bandha, Kapalabhati, and Bhastrika, the locks and retentions of Mula Bandha and Bahya Kumbhaka, the cooling Sheetali, the warming Surya Bhedana, the throat work of Ujjayi, and the two belly-led practices, Dirga and diaphragmatic breathing.

Slow diaphragmatic breathing and reflux

This is the piece with real evidence. Eherer and colleagues randomised nineteen people with non-erosive reflux disease to four weeks of abdominal breathing training or to a control group. Acid exposure in the oesophagus fell from 9.1 percent of the time to 4.7 percent, quality of life improved, and the control group did not change. At nine months, only the eleven people who had kept practising still showed the benefit, and their weekly acid-suppressing medication had dropped. The sample is very small, people with a large hiatal hernia were excluded, and only the abstract is openly readable.

Halland and colleagues later showed the mechanism directly with manometry: pressure at the lower oesophageal sphincter rose during inspiratory diaphragmatic breathing, and reflux events after a meal fell. Their honest finding is the one worth holding onto, since total acid exposure over forty-eight hours did not differ from sham. The practice appears to help around meals rather than all day. Qiu and colleagues pooled seven studies and found sphincter pressure the single outcome consistent enough to combine.

Ong and colleagues found a clear benefit in a narrower group: in people whose reflux came with supragastric belching, diaphragmatic breathing training reduced belching in sixty percent of them against none in the comparison group, and the effect held at four months.

How to practise it

The protocol in these trials is plain, and it is close to Dirga and to the low, slow breath underneath most of this page. We sit or lie with one hand on the belly. The inhale moves the hand outward as the diaphragm descends, the chest stays quiet, and the exhale is slow and a little longer than the inhale. Twenty to thirty minutes a day in the trials, often split into shorter sessions, and the reflux work was done sitting upright after meals. The nine-month follow-up matters: the benefit belonged to the people who kept practising.

What stays traditional

Kapalabhati, Bhastrika, Agnisar Kriya, Nauli, Uddiyana Bandha, Mula Bandha, and Bahya Kumbhaka are all taught as digestive practices, and almost none of them has been tested. The one exception is small: Minvaleev and colleagues measured blood flow in the superior mesenteric artery in twelve healthy volunteers before and after a hundred repetitions of agnisara and found it rose. That is circulation to the gut rather than digestion, measured in one group with no control, so it is a hint about mechanism and nothing more. The laboratory work on Kapalabhati measures autonomic indices rather than digestion, and several of those studies sit in weakly indexed journals. The claim that twists, folds, or abdominal practices change gut motility, transit time, or gastric emptying has not been measured in a yoga trial; it is inference from heart rate variability findings. None of that makes the practices useless. It makes the digestive label a traditional one, which is how this page means it.

For yoga as a whole and irritable bowel syndrome, Saab and colleagues pooled eleven randomised trials of 535 people in 2025 and found no statistically significant effect on symptom severity, with high risk of bias across the studies. The NIH complementary health centre puts it plainly: some research links yoga to improvement in people with irritable bowel syndrome, and there is not enough high-quality evidence to draw conclusions.

Care with the forceful practices

The cautions usually attached to Kapalabhati, Nauli, Uddiyana Bandha, and strong retention, which name pregnancy, hernia, ulcers, recent abdominal surgery, reflux, and eating disorders, come from the teaching tradition rather than from clinical research, and I have not found a medical source that states them. I pass them on as the tradition’s caution and not as evidence. Two things do come from research: a review of reported yoga injuries advises beginners to avoid forceful breathing and to learn under a qualified teacher, and the reflux manometry work found that straining of the Valsalva kind provoked reflux in most people with upright reflux disease, which is worth knowing before adding breath holds and strong abdominal locks.
