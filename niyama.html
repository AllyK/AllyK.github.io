THE SECOND LIMB OF YOGA
Niyama
the inner observances · Yoga Sutra 2.32

Turning inward

The five inner observances

The niyamas are the second limb of yoga, the inner observances. Where the yamas point outward to how we meet the world, the niyamas turn toward how we tend ourselves. Donna Farhi frames the second limb by who is watching: the niyamas are “how we live when no one else is watching,” a testing ground for “whether our private and public lives are congruent and that we walk our talk.” In sutra 2.32 Patanjali names five niyamas: śauca (purity), santoṣa (contentment), tapas (disciplined effort), svādhyāya (self-study), and īśvara-praṇidhāna (surrender to the divine). The niyamas are tools for cultivating daily steadiness and self-confidence. The Sanskrit scholar Edwin Bryant draws the line between the two limbs precisely: “In contrast with the yamas, which are concerned with how the yogī interacts with others, the niyamas are centered on one’s own personal discipline and practice.”

The niyamas flow together and with the other limbs of yoga. Purity clears the mind, contentment settles it, disciplined effort tempers it, self-study turns it toward its own depths, and surrender releases it into something larger. The five build toward the last, which this site takes up at length under The Divine.

ON SOURCES

This page draws on Deborah Adele’s The Yamas & Niyamas: Exploring Yoga’s Ethical Practice (On-Word Bound Books, 2009) and on Maetreyii Ma’s Living Love: The Yoga of Yama and Niyama (Ananda Gurukula, 2016), alongside the sutras themselves. Translations of the niyamas vary, and different lineages weigh them differently. Where a reading is one interpretation among several, it is offered as that.

The five niyamas

Each with its sutra, its meaning, and its practice
Sauchaśauca
Purity of body and mind
Tap to open the teaching
Sattva-śuddhi-saumanasya-aikāgrya-indriya-jaya-ātma-darśana-yogyatvāni ca. From purity of heart follow cheerfulness, one-pointedness, mastery of the senses, and the fitness to know the Self.YOGA SUTRA 2.41

Saucha is the practice of cleansing, of both the body and the mind. Maetreyii Ma describes it as clearing away the clutter that settles on the mind so that a clearer seeing becomes possible, the way a cleaned window lets in more light. The practice extends to choosing wisely among the food, the emotions, and the thoughts we let in, since each of these becomes part of us. Purity is about caring, tending, and holding ourselves lightly to help us clear away what doesn’t serve us. David Frawley, writing from an Ayurvedic view in Yoga and Ayurveda, gives saucha a more literal edge, counting vegetarian diet and physical detoxification among its practices and placing the niyamas at the base of Ayurveda’s daily regimens for balancing the constitution.

IN PRACTICE

In movement, saucha is a clean, attentive practice: a clear space, an unhurried breath, the body cared for rather than pushed. In meditation, we settle the running commentary in our minds. In daily life, we consider what we consume, the food, the images, and choose what leaves us clearer.

Deborah Adele: The Yamas & Niyamas Donna Farhi: Bringing Yoga to Life David Frawley: Yoga and Ayurveda
Santoshasantoṣa
Contentment
Tap to open the teaching
Santoṣād anuttamaḥ sukha-lābhaḥ. From contentment comes unsurpassed happiness.YOGA SUTRA 2.42

Santosha is contentment and acceptance of our life and ourselves as we are in this moment. It is the recognition that we already have whatever we need inside ourselves, which makes happiness a practice rather than a circumstance we wait on. Maetreyii Ma pairs it with saucha as the clear waters of the mind, and names the barrier plainly: when we are wrapped up in our own fears and wants, even a calm mind cannot see to its own depths. A close modern reading is Tara Brach’s radical acceptance, which she calls the willingness to experience ourselves and our life as it is: clearly recognizing what is present and regarding it “with an open, kind and loving heart,” the two wings she names as mindfulness and compassion. It is, she is careful to say, neither resignation nor self-indulgence, but the ground from which change becomes possible. Edwin Bryant, glossing the sutra, explains why the tradition calls this happiness unsurpassed: it is “a sāttvic happiness” that “does not depend on external objects, which are vulnerable and fleeting, but is inherent in the mind when it is tranquil and contented.” Contentment on this reading is not something we manufacture but a joy already native to a settled mind, uncovered once the hankering falls away.

IN PRACTICE

In movement, santosha meets the body it has today, without measuring it against yesterday, against an imagined “should,” or against anyone else. In meditation, it lets the breath be enough. In daily life, it loosens the grip of comparing and craving, and lets ourselves be enough in our work, not something we have to prove or earn.

Deborah Adele: The Yamas & Niyamas Donna Farhi: Bringing Yoga to Life Tara Brach: Radical Acceptance · Bantam
Tapastapas
Disciplined effort
Tap to open the teaching
Kāya-indriya-siddhir aśuddhi-kṣayāt tapasaḥ. Through disciplined effort, impurity dwindles and the body and senses are refined.YOGA SUTRA 2.43

Tapas means the heat and warmth generated by steady effort that turns intention into action. Maetreyii Ma suggests tapas focuses energy through love, burning away the small, protective sense of self in the fire of a larger devotion of our choosing. Maya Tiwari traces the word to its root sense of heat or glow: in The Path of Practice she treats tapas as the observance of yogic disciplines that kindles inner vitality, and extends it to speech through vac tapasya, a practice of noticing and refining one’s habitual thoughts and words rather than voicing them raw. Edwin Bryant grounds the classical sense in plain endurance: tapas, he writes, is “the ability to tolerate hunger and thirst as well as all the dualities of life (hot and cold, etc.), to avoid useless talk, and to perform fasts.” Its fruit, named in the next sutra (2.43), is that impurity dwindles and the body and senses are refined.

IN PRACTICE

In movement, tapas is the sustainable effort that shows up on the mat on ordinary days. In meditation, it is the discipline of returning, without frustration or self-criticism, to the breath. In daily life, we focus on changes we can actually keep, and meeting mundane tasks with full attention, so that discipline is our practice and not a burden.

Deborah Adele: The Yamas & Niyamas Donna Farhi: Bringing Yoga to Life Maya Tiwari: The Path of Practice
Svadhyayasvādhyāya
Self-study
Tap to open the teaching
Svādhyāyād iṣṭa-devatā-samprayogaḥ. Through self-study, one draws near the chosen ideal.YOGA SUTRA 2.44

In Sanskrit, svadhyaya joins sva, the self, with a root meaning to reflect, study, and contemplate, so the niyama holds both the study of sacred texts of our choosing and the study of ourselves, a seeking to understand the core of our being. Maetreyii Ma reads it as reflection upon the nature of the soul, the Atman, and self-knowledge. A text read closely returns us to ourselves, and honest self-observation sends us back to the text.

IN PRACTICE

In movement, svadhyaya is honest witnessing of the body, what feels good and opening and what feels tight or resistant. In meditation, it is self-inquiry, watching the mind to begin to know it. In daily life, it pairs the study of texts that steady us with observation of our own patterns, and asks whether the way we are living matches what we value. The teacher Jacoby Ballard offers a lived example: meeting a hard critique of his own teaching, he treats it as “an opportunity to practice self-reflection, or svadhyaya, one of the Niyamas of the eight limbs of Raja Yoga,” choosing honest self-examination over defensiveness. Patanjali gives the definition; this is one contemporary teacher showing what it looks like in the moment.

Ishvara Pranidhanaīśvara-praṇidhāna
Surrender to the divine
Tap to open the teaching
Samādhi-siddhir īśvara-praṇidhānāt. From surrender to the divine comes the attainment of samadhi.YOGA SUTRA 2.45

Ishvara pranidhana is surrender to the divine, a releasing of our attempts to control every outcome in favor of our larger purpose. It is the Bhagavad Gita’s teaching stated plainly: you have a right to your action, but never to its fruits (2.47). Maetreyii Ma describes it as self-surrender in deep contemplation of the eternal source of all, and notes that people have named that source differently across cultures and time, while pointing toward a divinity, oneness, connection, or consciousness greater than our individual sense of self. B.K.S. Iyengar makes the same point about the word itself: this is “the most theistic of all aspects of yoga,” yet “Isvara is Divinity in a general and nondenominational sense,” not the god of any one tradition. The grouping is Patanjali’s own: Yoga Sutra 2.1 defines kriya yoga, the yoga of action, as tapas, svadhyaya, and ishvara pranidhana together. David Frawley, following that sutra, notes that these last three niyamas form Kriya Yoga, the yoga of internal action that readies one for samadhi, and that surrender is what sustains the rest, since these disciplines cannot be carried by personal effort alone. This site takes up that question, and the many doors into it, at length under The Divine. Edwin Bryant marks what sets this last niyama apart: of all the fruits the sutras assign to the yamas and niyamas, from clarity of body to mystic powers, “it is only from Īśvara-praṇidhāna that samādhi, the actual and absolute goal of yoga, is attained.” The other results stay within the changing realm of nature; surrender alone reaches the goal. The text is explicitly theistic here, though a practitioner may hold ishvara as surrender to a chosen ideal rather than to any one tradition’s God.

IN PRACTICE

In movement, ishvara pranidhana offers the practice rather than performs it, letting the effort be given rather than displayed. In meditation, it is the letting go into something vast enough to hold us, the release that the deepest absorption asks for. In daily life, it echoes the Gita’s core instruction: to do the work fully and release our claim on its fruits, trusting what we cannot control to something larger than ourselves.

Further reading

The books behind this page, and the peer-reviewed work on the observant limbs

The books this page draws on, alongside the peer-reviewed work on the observant limbs, which remain among the least studied in modern research.

The Yamas & Niyamas
Deborah Adele · On-Word Bound Books
One chapter per observance: saucha as care rather than perfection, santosha as the recognition that there is enough.
Bringing Yoga to Life
Donna Farhi · HarperOne
The niyamas as daily practice, held inside the rhythm of effort and surrender.
Living Love: The Yoga of Yama and Niyama
Maetreyii Ma · Ananda Gurukula
A practitioner’s book-length treatment of all ten yamas and niyamas as teachings for daily life; the source here for tapas as focused love and svadhyaya as reflection on the soul. A teaching text rather than a study, and offered as one.
Body Thrive
Cate Stillman · Sounds True
Dinacharya as habit; the niyamas turned into a daily rhythm the body can keep.
Impact of yama and niyama on psychospiritual factors in young adults: a randomized controlled trial
XU, ITAGI & THAIYAR · INT. J. YOGA (PHILOSOPHY, PSYCHOLOGY & PARAPSYCHOLOGY), 2021
One hundred young adults, randomized to three months of yama-niyama training or control. The training group gained on sattva and dropped on rajas and tamas (Vedic Personality Inventory, P<0.001). Early evidence that the observant limbs are trainable, with measurable change in disposition.
From Yama to Samyama
INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF YOGA, 2016 · 9(2), 95–96
Argues that the eight limbs are meant to be practiced together rather than climbed in sequence, the basis of this site’s invitation to begin anywhere. The niyamas deepen the yamas rather than follow them.
Yoga and Ayurveda: Self-Healing and Self-Realization
David Frawley · Lotus Press
Reads the niyamas through Ayurveda, as the base of daily regimens for balancing the constitution. Frawley lists them in a different order than Patanjali does here, contentment first, and gives saucha a literal cast of diet and detoxification; the last three he groups together as Kriya Yoga. A framing from one tradition among several.
The Path of Practice
Maya Tiwari · Penguin Random House
A woman’s book of healing with food, breath, and sound. Read here for tapas as the kindling of inner vitality and for vac tapasya, the discipline of noticing and refining one’s speech. A teaching text rather than a study.
