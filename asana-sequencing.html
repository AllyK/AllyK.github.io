Sequencing Asana

vinyasa krama · the intelligent ordering of practice

How a class is built: the arc that warms, builds, peaks, and returns the body to rest

What sequencing is

Order is the teaching

A yoga class is not a set of poses. It is an order of poses, and the order is where most of the teaching lives. Two classes can hold every posture in common and still land in different bodies and different states of mind, decided only by what came before what. This page is about that order. It is not a place to collect classes; it sets out the principles I sequence by and the sources they come from, so you can build a practice rather than borrow one.

The old name for this intelligence is vinyasa krama. T.K.V. Desikachar breaks the words apart plainly: krama is the step, and nyasa means to place, so vinyasa krama is a correctly organized course of practice in which it is not enough to take a step, the step has to be placed in the right direction. Mark Stephens, whose Yoga Sequencing is the standard modern treatment, reads it the same way and pairs it with parinamavada, the understanding that change is constant and always builds on what came just before. From those two ideas he draws the plainest rule of ordering there is: move from the simple to the complex along the path of least resistance. Both men trace the concept to Tirumalai Krishnamacharya, whose students Desikachar, Indra Devi, B.K.S. Iyengar, and Pattabhi Jois carried it into the practice most of the world now inherits.

It helps to remember where asana sits in the older map. In Patanjali’s eight limbs, posture is the third limb and breath the fourth, and the order of the limbs is itself a sequence. A steady, unstrained seat, sthira sukham asanam in Yoga Sutra 2.46, is what makes the breath quiet enough to work with, and quiet breath is what lets the senses and the mind turn inward. Read this way, an asana class is preparation. Its arc is meant to leave the body settled enough that breath, the withdrawal of the senses, and focus can actually happen. Sequencing that forgets this can build a strong body and a restless mind, which is close to the opposite of the point.

The shape of a class

The arc: warm, build, peak, descend, rest

A well-built class has the shape of a wave. Stephens names five stages in the vinyasa krama arc, and they are the frame worth learning first: initiating the practice, warming the body, the pathway to the peak, the peak itself, and integration. Stephens contrasts three common ways a class gets built: pure improvisation, a fixed set series like the Ashtanga primary sequence, and template teaching. Each has a cost. His answer, and the one this page follows, is intentional sequencing, where anatomy, energetics, and the actual students in the room decide the order rather than habit or a fixed list. He notes plainly that a set series repeated without built-in counterposes, the Ashtanga primary calls for flowing through Chaturanga more than fifty times, can itself cause strain however well aligned each repetition is.

The arc is not only the climb. Desikachar is insistent that a practice has to bring you back. It is not enough to climb the tree, he writes, remembering a brother stuck at the top of a coconut palm with no idea how to get down; you have to be able to come down too. A class that reaches its hardest pose and simply stops has told half a story. The poses that unwind the peak, and the rest at the end, are as much a part of the krama as the ones that built it. Amber Burke’s survey of alternative structures is a useful reminder that the peak-pose wave is one shape among several: poses can be repeated with new entrances, a path retraced in reverse, floor and standing work alternated. The arc is the grammar to learn first, and like any grammar it can be played with once it is in the body.

The principles

What decides the order

These are the principles I hold every sequence to. None of them is mine. Each traces to a teacher or a text, and I have named the source so you can read past my summary of it.

Warm before you load, simple before complex

Breath and small movements warm and open the spine before more intense work asks anything of the rest of the body. Stephens states the underlying rule as moving progressively from simple to complex actions along the path of least resistance, so that the deepest shape of a practice is also its easiest by the time it arrives. Standing poses are the safest family for general warming, grounding and heating the body while the harder work is still being rehearsed quietly in the background.

Sequence toward a state, not just a shape

Every order of poses produces an energetic result, and you can aim it. Stephens reads hatha itself as a balance, the ha more heating and the tha more cooling, and points out that the same class can be tilted stimulating or calming by which practices you choose and where you place them. A student who came in anxious and a student who came in flat need the order pitched differently. This is Krishnamacharya’s oldest tenet, that practice is fitted to the time, the place, and the person in front of you, and it is why I plan for the room rather than from a template. The lineages page traces where that tenet came from.

Every deep action is neutralized, not merely reversed

A deep action leaves tension behind, and the next poses have to settle it before the practice moves on. How to do that is a real disagreement between two teachers I trust, so I have set it out in its own note below rather than pretend it is settled. The counterposes for every pose in the library are mapped in the family tree’s anatomy data, and the descent of a class is built from them.

Every transition is written, and the body must be able to get there

A sequence is a list of journeys, and the journeys deserve rows of their own. Before a plan is final I trace the body through every consecutive pair of poses and ask whether the movement is physically possible. Lizard Pose straight into Happy Baby fails that test; there is no honest path from one to the other without intermediate steps. A transition left unwritten is a decision left to the most tired moment of the class, so between pose families, and especially between standing and floor work, a vinyasa is written in as its own labeled step to carry the body across the gap and reset the breath.

The descent and the rest are the practice, not the epilogue

Whatever the peak gathered, the descent releases, and then the class rests below the level of effort where it began. Stephens calls Savasana by far the most important asana in the whole practice, the place where everything is finally integrated, and notes that the settled state just before and after it is the ideal ground for pranayama and meditation. The arc does not really end at the summit; it ends in stillness, pointed at the limbs beyond asana.

A live disagreement · how to counterpose

Desikachar teaches the literal counterpose: a powerful forward bend is followed by a gentle back bend, a powerful back bend by a simple forward bend, and a headstand by a shoulderstand to relieve the pressure that would otherwise, in his words, deteriorate or misalign the vertebrae of the neck. Stephens argues that the literal reading is often the wrong one. Following a headstand with Mountain Pose, its true opposite, can leave a student dizzy and falling; the real work of pratikriyasana, action against accumulated tension, is usually done with a similar and simpler pose that releases the strain, and is best planned across the whole class rather than pose by pose. I lean toward Stephens for the middle of a practice and toward Desikachar’s caution around the neck and the deep joints, and I tell students which one I am doing and why. Where the two teachers differ, I would rather show the seam than paper over it.

The summit and the way down

Reading a peak backward

A peak-pose class is read from its summit down. Choose the peak, name what it physically demands, and the first two thirds of the class assemble those demands one at a time.

Firefly Pose asks for open hamstrings, deep hip flexion, and shoulder-pressing strength, so a class built toward it opens the hamstrings in waves, floor first and standing second, and rehearses the shoulder work in stages long before the full pose appears. A forearm balance asks for shoulder stability and a settled relationship with being upside down, so dolphin and forearm plank belong in the opening minutes, quietly rehearsing the peak while the body still thinks it is only warming up. By the time the summit arrives nothing about it is new; the class has already practiced every piece of it separately. This is the pathway-to-the-peak stage of the arc, and it is mostly patience.

The way down mirrors the way up. Whatever the peak gathered, the descent releases: folds after deep hip work, gentle flexion after backbends, wrist and shoulder release after arm balances, symmetry after asymmetry. Stephens is precise that this integration is sequenced with as much care as the climb, moving from simple to complex on the way back down before settling into the calming inversions, the seated folds, and finally Savasana. The nervous system files the whole journey as one completed shape.

Sequencing and safety

The order is also the safety

Most of what keeps a practice safe is not a cue inside a pose; it is the order of the poses around it. A joint asked for its end range while still cold, or a fragile structure loaded at the peak of fatigue, is a sequencing failure before it is an alignment one.

Two facts from the muscle diagram shape how I order deep work. First, as Bernie Clark sets out, how far a given body can travel into a pose is often fixed by the shape of its own bones, the point of compression where one bone meets another, not by effort or tightness. That means a sequence should open range gradually and read a stall as information, not as a target to force. Second, the neck is the structure to protect most carefully in the order of a class. Desikachar warns that a headstand practiced without care for the neck can, over time, jam and deteriorate the cervical vertebrae, which is why he counterposes it; William Broad, gathering the medical case literature, records the rarer and graver risk that extreme neck loading in shoulderstand, headstand, and deep backbends has been tied to vertebral-artery injury and stroke. The practical lesson for sequencing is the same from both: warm the shoulders and spine thoroughly first, keep the neck long and unforced, place inversions and deep backbends well inside the arc rather than early or when the body is spent, and give them their counterposes. The full anatomy, with its sources, lives on the muscle diagram.

Where the arc points

A class is a small cycle

The arc of a single class, gathering and then releasing, returning the body lower than it began, is a small version of the cycles the tradition tells in its stories: creation and dissolution, effort and surrender, the going out and the coming home. The steadiness and ease that Patanjali asks of a single pose, sthira and sukha, is the same balance the older texts read into the whole moving world. Those stories, and the poses paired with them, live under the banyan tree. Sequencing well is finally just this: to build an order that a body can climb, that a body can come down from, and that leaves a person more settled walking out of the room than they were walking in.
